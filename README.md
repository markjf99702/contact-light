# Contact Light

**Play it: [contact-light.junkdrawer.works](https://contact-light.junkdrawer.works/)**

**Land on the Moon the way the Apollo commanders did: a foot per second at a time.** Each landing starts at low gate, about 500 feet up and still moving forward, and something is wrong with the spot the computer picked: the boulders round West crater, the middle of Surveyor crater, a slope at Hadley. You take over in P66, the program every crew landed in. The computer holds whatever descent rate you click, you tilt the LM to slow down or fly on, and the LM pilot calls your numbers the way Aldrin did ("40 feet, down 2 1/2. Picking up some dust.") while Houston counts down the fuel. Then the probes touch, the blue lunar contact lamp comes on, and you hear the real words from that landing.

## How it plays

- **The rate switch** (▲ ▼, or the ↑ ↓ keys): each click changes the descent rate the computer holds by a foot per second. It starts wherever you were coming down when you took over, so the first thing to do is usually click it up a few times.
- **The hand controller** (drag it, or hold ← →): drag back to tilt back and slow down, forward to speed up. Let go and the LM stands upright again and coasts. Two thumbs: one on the rate switch, one on the controller.
- **The numbers** across the top are altitude, how fast you're coming down, speed along the ground and fuel. The strip under them is the whole site in profile, with you, the target and what the screen is showing. Below 100 feet, marks on the ground show where the footpads will come down, and the angle there: green is good, amber is rough, red is no.
- **The dust.** Below about 100 feet the engine blasts the surface out sideways, grains racing off in every direction, and near the ground it hides the surface the way it did for the crews (Apollo 12 and 15 landed "on the gauges"). When the engine stops it's gone at once: nothing hangs in a vacuum. The LM's shadow, out ahead of you all the way down because the Sun is low behind you, slides back to meet the footpads.
- **Fuel.** The low-level light comes on at 5.6%. Houston starts the clock: 94 seconds to Bingo, with calls at 60 and 30 seconds, and at Bingo you have 20 seconds to land or abort. Run dry or run out the clock up high and it's abort stage: the ascent stage leaves without you.
- **The radio.** The LM pilot calls your height, rate and speed along the ground, and says so when something's wrong: coming down too fast, moving too fast along the ground to stop before the footpads touch, or flying on past the target. "Looking good" means it is.
- **Contact light** means one of the 67-inch probes under the footpads has touched. Hold steady: the engine stops when the footpads touch.
- **Plaques.** Every touchdown number gets gold, silver or bronze: how fast you came down and along the ground, how upright you were, the slope you came to rest on, rocks under the footpads, distance from the target, and whether you were down before Houston's 30-second call. The plaque is the worst of them, so the table says exactly what to fly better. Past the LM's limits (10 ft/s down, less when you're moving along the ground; 4 ft/s along it; a 12° slope) it crashes or tips over, and the card names everything that went wrong. Each number is judged as the card shows it, to a tenth.
- **The loop.** Each result keeps the radio from your landing, stamped the way the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal prints it: mission time from the moment that crew took over in P66. Fly Apollo 11 the way Armstrong did and your "Contact light" lands at 102:45:40 too; the card says when theirs came on.
- **Training** at Ellington, in the jet-lifted LLTV every commander flew, teaches the controls one at a time, the rate switch and hand controller in P66 or the throttle and rotation in Arcade. It's marked Start here until you've landed there, and every briefing names the two controls in a line.
- **Arcade** is the 1979 way: raw throttle and rotation and no computer holding you up. It keeps its own plaques. **Free flight** rolls new ground with something wrong at the target; the autopilot lands every roll before you get it, so it can be done.
- **Read calls aloud** has the browser's own voice speak the radio, if it has one. Voices vary from phone to phone.
- No account and no server. Your plaques stay in your browser. It works offline and installs to a phone's home screen.

## The sites

- **Ellington.** The Lunar Landing Training Vehicle over a Texas airfield: hover, fly to the pad, stop and set it down.
- **Apollo 11, Tranquility Base.** The computer is taking you into the boulder field around West crater, about 540 feet across. Fly past it and find smooth ground, as Armstrong did, with less fuel to spare than any crew after him.
- **Apollo 12, Surveyor crater.** The target is the middle of a 650-foot crater, with Surveyor 3 on its far slope. Fly over and land on the rim near the old probe.
- **Apollo 14, Fra Mauro.** Hummocky ground and the Triplet craters short of the planned spot. Shepard flew on over them and landed on a 7° slope.
- **Apollo 15, Hadley.** A plain pocked with small craters, sloping toward Hadley Rille, with the Apennine mountains behind and the thickest dust of all. Scott landed straddling a crater rim and Falcon sat at 11°.
- **Apollo 16, Descartes.** Highland ground, craters on craters and blocks the size of cars.
- **Apollo 17, Taurus–Littrow.** A valley between mountains taller than the Grand Canyon is deep. You come down fast, toward small craters short of Camelot.

## Where the numbers come from

- **The LM.** Moon gravity is 5.32 ft/s². The descent engine throttles from 1,050 to 10,125 pounds of thrust, with a specific impulse of about 311 seconds; in P66 the computer used 10% to 60% of it. The LM is 31 feet across its footpads and about 23 tall, and its 67-inch contact probes hang from three of the four footpads: the one on the ladder leg was left off, in case a probe bent at touchdown tore a spacesuit on the way down. The landing gear was built for 10 ft/s straight down, or 7 ft/s down while moving 4 ft/s sideways, and certified for slopes up to 12° (Apollo Experience Report TN D-6722).
- **P66.** Each click of the rate-of-descent switch changed the commanded descent rate by a foot per second, and the computer throttled the engine to hold it. In the game the hand controller sets the tilt and the LM stands upright when you let go; the real one commanded a rate of turn and held whatever attitude it was left at.
- **The fuel calls.** The low-level sensor tripped at 5.6% of the descent propellant, and Houston counted 94 seconds from it to Bingo: land within 20 seconds or abort. Fuel loads in the game are set to make each landing tight but fair, not copied from the flights.
- **The radio.** The calls during your descent are built from your own numbers in each LM pilot's style, from the air-to-ground transcripts: Aldrin's "down 2 1/2", Bean's "coming down at", Irwin's terse "80 at 5". The lines after touchdown are the real ones, from the [Apollo Lunar Surface Journal](https://apollojournals.org/alsj/main.html). The beeps are Quindar tones, 2,525 Hz before and 2,475 Hz after a quarter of a second each; they keyed Houston's transmitters, which is why only Houston's lines have them.
- **The sites** are drawn after the real ones from the mission reports and the Journal, not measured from them: the big craters are about the right size and in about the right order along the approach, the Sun is about as high as it was that morning, and the small craters and rocks are made up. The game flies one axis, along your track, because a side view can't show drift across it.
- No NASA insignia or mission patches; the plaques are this game's own design. Nothing here is from or endorsed by NASA.

## Running it

It's a static site: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no build step.

```sh
npx serve .                   # or any static file server, then open the printed address
npm test                      # unit tests, then flies it in Chromium through the real page (needs Playwright)
node tools/make-icons.mjs     # redraws the PNG icons from icon.svg
```

To put it online with GitHub Pages: **Settings → Pages → Build and deployment → Deploy from a branch**, then pick `main` and `/ (root)`. The `CNAME` file puts it at contact-light.junkdrawer.works.

### Files

- `js/sim.js`: the flight model: Moon gravity, the descent engine, P66 holding the rate, the attitude jets, fuel and Houston's clock, contact and touchdown.
- `js/terrain.js`: the ground as a height every foot, craters, boulders, how the low Sun lights it, and how a lander would sit at any spot.
- `js/sites.js`: the seven sites, with their craters, rocks, crews, briefings and fuel.
- `js/judge.js`: gold, silver or bronze for each touchdown number, and what went wrong if it didn't land.
- `js/calls.js`: the radio: each crew's way of calling the numbers, Houston's calls and the real lines after touchdown.
- `js/autopilot.js`: a steady pilot that flies through the same controls you do; the tests use it, the title flies it, and Free flight checks new ground with it.
- `js/freeflight.js`: new ground from a number. `js/coach.js`: the hints at Ellington.
- `js/render.js` and `js/craft.js`: drawing the scene and the LM and LLTV; `js/strip.js`: the site in profile; `js/plaque.js`: the plaques.
- `js/sound.js`: every sound made in the browser with Web Audio, and the browser's voice. `js/store.js`: what's kept in localStorage (`contact-light.v1`).
- `js/main.js`: the page: screens, controls and the loop.
- `fonts/`: Jost and B612 Mono (SIL Open Font License), served from here so nothing loads from elsewhere. B612 was drawn for aircraft cockpit displays.
- `sw.js`: keeps a copy of the game for flying offline.
